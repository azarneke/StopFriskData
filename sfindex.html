Question 1 · Demographic analysis

Black New Yorkers are stopped at about 12 times the rate of white New Yorkers

What is the racial and gender distribution of people stopped and frisked in 2022? Are there disparities by race or gender?

Race. 8,863 of the 15,102 stops (58.7%) involved a person recorded as Black, 4,477 (29.6%) Hispanic, 1,077 (7.1%) white, 320 (2.1%) Asian / Pacific Islander and 173 (1.1%) another group. Against 2020 Census populations, that is 4.99 stops per 1,000 Black residents, 1.80 per 1,000 Hispanic residents and 0.40 per 1,000 white residents. White New Yorkers are 30.9% of the city but 7.1% of stops.

Gender. 91.6% of stops were of men (13,829) and 7.5% of women (1,139). Women were a larger share of white stops (13.5%) than of Black (7.0%) or Hispanic (7.6%) stops.

Question 2 · Temporal trends

Stops peak in spring and in the late evening; December and early mornings are quietest

How did stop-and-frisk encounters change over 2022's months? Are there days of the week or times of day when stops are more common?

Months. Activity was steady at roughly 1,100–1,170 stops in winter, rose to a spring plateau (March 1,437, April 1,460, May 1,471; about 48 per day in April), eased through summer, ticked up again in October (1,373) and fell to a low of 841 in December, 27 stops per day versus 49 in April.

Days. Wednesday was the busiest day (2,537 stops), then Saturday (2,416) and Thursday (2,308). Monday was the quietest (1,561).

Hours. 42% of stops (6,343) happened between 6 pm and midnight, peaking at 10 pm (1,207). A second bump follows at midnight–1 am (1,149 and 888), and the trough is 6–7 am (about 130 per hour). After midnight, calendar Saturday and Sunday are busiest (757 and 604 stops between 12 and 5 am, i.e. Friday and Saturday nights, vs. about 280 on Mondays and Tuesdays).

Question 3 · Reasons for stops

Nearly half of stops cite a suspected weapon, and those stops rarely end in an arrest

What reasons do officers most often give? Is there a relationship between the stated reason and the outcome?

Suspected crime. Criminal possession of a weapon (CPW) is the stated suspicion in 6,908 stops, 45.7% of all stops. Robbery (1,544; 10.2%), petit larceny (1,336; 8.8%), assault (1,269; 8.4%) and burglary (1,077; 7.1%) follow; the top five reasons cover 80% of stops.

Observed circumstances. On the form's checklist, officers most often cite "fits a description" (51.5% of stops), "proximity to a crime scene" (42.3%) and a suspected concealed weapon (33.5%).

Reason vs. outcome. The reason strongly predicts what happens. Weapon-suspicion stops end in an arrest only 20.7% of the time (75.8% end with no arrest or summons) even though 87% involve a frisk. Property-crime stops have the opposite profile: petit larceny stops end in arrest 61.4% of the time and robbery stops 46.4%. Self-initiated stops are less likely to end in arrest (23.0%) than radio runs (38.9%) or stops on a victim's complaint at the scene (36.9%).

Question 4 · Outcomes of stops

About one stop in three ends in an arrest; frisk rates, not arrest rates, are what differ by race

What percentage of stops end in arrests, summonses or other outcomes? Do outcomes differ by demographics?

Overall. 33.0% of stops (4,985) ended in an arrest and 2.8% (420) in a summons (53 stops had both); 64.6% (9,750) ended with neither. In 60% of stops the person was frisked and in 42% searched.

By race. Arrest rates are close for Black (32.9%), Hispanic (32.5%) and Asian (35.3%) people, and somewhat higher for white people (37.7%). The big differences are in how the encounter went before that: 63.5% of Black and 60.6% of Hispanic people stopped were frisked, versus 33.2% of white people and 46.6% of Asian people. Summonses were issued in 3.0% of Black stops and 1.4% of white stops.

By gender. Women were frisked far less often (35.5% vs. 61.9%) but arrested slightly more often (36.4% vs. 32.9%). A firearm was drawn in 4.6% of all stops, with little variation among the three largest groups (4.2–4.6%).

Arrest rates alone cannot show whether the bar for stopping people differs by group. What the file does show is that frisks, the more intrusive step, are applied unevenly even though weapon hit rates among those frisked are similar (see question 6).

Question 5 · Location analysis

The Bronx and Brooklyn account for 59% of stops; one Bronx precinct alone for 7.6%

Which neighbourhoods or precincts have the most stops? Is there a correlation between where stops occur and the demographics of the population?

Precincts. Stops are recorded in all 77 precincts but are concentrated: the 46th Precinct (Bronx) logged 1,143, more than double the next precinct (75th, Brooklyn: 502). Then the 48th (471), 40th (437) and 43rd (436), all in the Bronx. The top five precincts account for 19.8% of stops and the top fifteen for 40.7%.

Boroughs. The Bronx (4,495 stops; 3.05 per 1,000 residents) and Brooklyn (4,465; 1.63) lead in count; relative to population, the Bronx is highest, followed by Manhattan (1.98), Brooklyn, Queens (0.98) and Staten Island (0.89).

Demographic correlation. Across the five boroughs the stop rate rises with the Hispanic share of residents (r = +0.82) and Black share (r = +0.67) and falls with the white share (r = −0.61). With only five data points this is suggestive, not conclusive. Within every borough, Black residents are over-represented among those stopped: in Brooklyn they are 26.7% of residents but 68.6% of stops, in Manhattan 11.8% vs. 58.4%.

Question 6 · Weapon and contraband discovery

A weapon turned up in 1 of every 6 stops, and in 1 of every 5 frisks or searches

How often do officers find weapons or contraband? Is discovery connected to the reason for the stop?

Frequency. A weapon was recovered in 2,411 stops (16.0%): a firearm in 1,187 (7.9%), a knife or cutting instrument in 1,083 (7.2%) and another weapon in 265 (1.8%). Other contraband was found in 875 stops (5.8%). Weapon or contraband together: 3,020 stops (20.0%). Among the 11,315 stops with a frisk or search, a weapon was found in 20.3%.

Link to reason. Stops for suspected weapon possession are the most productive: a weapon was found in 24.4% of them (firearm 15.0%) versus 8.8% of all other stops, and they account for 69.9% of all weapons recovered. Yet three in four (75.8%) still ended without an arrest or summons. Property-crime stops (petit larceny, burglary) yield weapons only 4–5% of the time but turn up other contraband more often (8–12%).

By race. Among people who were frisked, a weapon was found in 20.9% of Black, 21.6% of Hispanic, 19.0% of white and 24.2% of Asian stops. The hit rates are similar even though frisk rates differ widely, which is the central tension in the data.

Question 7 · Repeat encounters

This file cannot say how many people were stopped more than once

How many individuals were stopped multiple times in 2022? Are there patterns in the demographics or outcomes of repeat encounters?

Not answerable directly. The public file has one row per stop (STOP_ID) and no person identifier, so the same individual cannot be followed across stops. Anything below is an indicative proxy only and should not be quoted as a count of repeat stops.

Proxy. Among 12,506 stops with a complete physical description (age, sex, race, height, weight, build, hair and eye colour), 10,404 distinct descriptions appear. 3,324 stops (26.6%) share an identical description with at least one other 2022 stop; 802 descriptions occur twice, 215 three times, and the most repeated one 16 times. Many of these are surely different people who look alike on paper, and genuine repeat stops recorded with slightly different details would be missed, so the true figure could be higher or lower.

Pattern in the proxy. Matching descriptions are concentrated among Black and Hispanic men: 33.7% of Black and 21.1% of Hispanic stops match another stop, versus 3.5% of white stops. Arrest rates are similar for matched and unmatched stops (33.4% vs. 36.6%). Only 68 descriptions recur on the same calendar day, the likelier duplicates of one encounter.

What would answer it. A person-level identifier, even a pseudonymised one, would allow counting stops per person, the share stopped 2+ times and the outcome of each successive stop.

Question 8 · Age and stop frequency

Stops skew young: the median age is 26, and young people are stopped mainly on weapon suspicion

Is there a relationship between age and likelihood of being stopped? How do reasons differ by age group?

Distribution. Of 13,379 stops with a plausible recorded age (1,723 have none), the median is 26; 43% of people stopped were under 25 and 14.7% under 18 (1,967 stops). Counts peak at age 20 (821 stops). Per capita, people under 18 are stopped at about 1.1 per 1,000 residents of that age group; for adults the figure is about 1.6 per 1,000 (counting only stops with a known age, so both are slight underestimates). Under-18s are 19.8% of NYC residents but 14.7% of stops with a known age.

Reasons by age. Weapon suspicion dominates for the young: CPW is the stated reason for 51% of 15–17 year-olds and 60% of 18–24 year-olds, falling to 18% for people 55 and over. Older people are stopped mainly for property crimes: petit larceny is 22.5% of stops for 55+ versus 2.5% for 18–24 year-olds, and burglary rises from 4% to 14%. Robbery peaks for teenagers (20%).

Outcomes by age. Frisk and weapon-recovery rates peak at ages 18–24 (72% frisked; 22% weapon found) and decline with age, while arrest rates rise from about 31% for 18–24 year-olds to 43% for people over 45.

Question 9 · Officer identification

Individual officers aren't identified, but the busiest commands are, and outcomes vary widely between them

Which officers conducted the most stops? Do outcomes vary by officer?

Not answerable at officer level. The file contains the command of the issuing officer (199 distinct codes, many matching a precinct number) and the officer's rank, but no officer name or ID. The analysis below is therefore by command and by rank.

Most active commands. Command 46 recorded 1,234 stops (8.2% of all), about three times the next command (75: 406). The ten busiest commands account for 26.2% of stops and the top fifteen for 33.7%. 82% of stops were issued by police officers of rank POM, 11% by POF, and 3% by sergeants.

Variation in outcomes. Among the 88 commands with at least 50 stops, the arrest rate ranges from 11.3% to 67.6% (middle half: 27.2%–43.9%), the share of stops with no arrest or summons from 31.5% to 86.1%, and the weapon recovery rate from 3.1% to 69.8%. Command 46 has the most stops and the lowest arrest rate of any command with 50+ stops (11.3%; 86.1% end with no arrest or summons). By rank, outcomes are close: arrest rates are 28–35% for each rank with 100+ stops.

Differences between commands reflect in part what each area's stops are for (weapon-suspicion stops end in arrests less often), so they are a prompt for questions rather than a measure of individual officer behaviour.

Question 10 · Comparative analysis

2022 is the highest year since 2015, but still about 2% of the 2011 peak

How does 2022 compare with previous years in overall numbers and demographics? Have practices or outcomes changed?

Source note. The 2022 file has no earlier years, so the 2011–2021 figures below come from NYCLU's published tables and the Data Collaborative for Justice's 2022 summary (linked in the notes). 2022 values are computed from this file. Racial groupings are assumed to match NYCLU's (Latinx = Hispanic incl. Black Hispanic).

Volume. Stops fell from 685,724 in 2011 to 11,008 in 2018, rose to 13,459 in 2019, dropped to 9,544 in 2020 and 8,947 in 2021, then jumped 69% to 15,102 in 2022, the highest level since 2015. The Bronx doubled (2,368 to 4,495, +90%).

Demographics. The Black share of stops was 53% in 2011 and 59–60% in 2019 and 2021; in 2022 it is 58.7%. Hispanic share (29.6%) is in the 27–31% range of recent years. The white share (7.1%) is the lowest on record in this series (8–12% in earlier years). The racial composition has thus been broadly stable for a decade while volume changed by a factor of 45.

Practice and outcomes. The share of stops with no arrest or summons ("found innocent" in NYCLU's wording) fell from 88% in 2011 to 61% in 2021 as stops became rarer and more targeted, then rose to 64.6% in 2022. The mix shifted toward weapon-suspicion stops: CPW was 12% of stops in 2013, 37.5% in 2021 and 45.7% in 2022, and 75.8% of CPW stops in 2022 ended with no arrest or summons (71.3% in 2021).

Notes, definitions and limits

  • Data. NYPD Stop, Question and Frisk file for calendar year 2022: 15,102 rows, one per stop, 82 columns. All figures are computed from the rows, with no sampling or weighting.
  • Outcome definitions. Arrest = SUSPECT_ARRESTED_FLAG; summons = SUMMONS_ISSUED_FLAG; no action = neither. Weapon found = WEAPON_FOUND_FLAG; contraband = OTHER_CONTRABAND_FLAG.
  • Reported, not verified. Race, age and sex are recorded by the officer. Stops not reported are not in the file, and a stop being recorded does not imply anything about its legality.
  • Disparity is not proof of cause. Comparing stops to residents ignores who is present in a place, crime rates and police deployment. The charts show gaps; they do not isolate reasons for them.
  • Benchmarks. Population: NYC Department of City Planning, 2020 Census (briefing booklet). Earlier-year counts and demographics: NYCLU stop-and-frisk data. 2021–2022 comparisons and CPW shares: Data Collaborative for Justice, 10 Highlights from the 2022 Data.
